# T04 — NFL totals and sides: weather, referees, rest/travel, injuries, key numbers

## What I did

Searched each named sub-topic separately — wind, rain, cold, referee crews,
rest/bye weeks, short-week/travel, QB injuries, and the 3/7/41/43/44/47 key
numbers — then specifically looked for the "other side" of each: replication,
decay, or refutation evidence, since the standing orders weight that as highly
as the original claim. T01 had already registered the general late-season
home-underdog bias (S003, Borghesi) and the Levitt price-to-exploit mechanism
(S009), so I did not re-register those even though Borghesi and Levitt came up
repeatedly again here. Pulled the `nfl_total`/`nfl_spread`/`nfl_moneyline`
schemas first — all three share one schema, which matters below. Added 10
entries (S027–S036).

## Overall picture

Weather is the strongest-evidenced sub-topic and also the one where the
original academic claim and a recent independent practitioner replication
disagree on *which* weather variable actually works. Borghesi (2008) bundles
heat, wind, and rain into one "the market underprices adverse weather" result.
A 2025 practitioner replication across ~7,000 games (1999-2025) tested the
pieces separately using *actual game-time* readings and found wind holds up
(15+ mph → 56% under) but cold alone does not (≤32°F → only 47% under, i.e.
if anything a lean the other way) — a direct, data-backed refutation of the
"cold games go under" folk belief that most practitioner content still repeats
uncritically. I registered both as separate entries (S027 wind, S028 cold)
specifically so the refutation doesn't get buried inside a bundled "weather
matters" claim. Rain has zero isolated data behind it anywhere I could find —
not in Borghesi's accessible excerpts, not in any practitioner replication —
and the dataset has no precipitation column at all, so it is registered
(S029) but explicitly marked untestable and flagged as the weakest-evidenced
third of the bundled weather claim.

Referee crews (S030) and the backup-QB timing edge (S034) are the two weakest
entries by evidence grade, and for the same underlying reason: every number I
could find is a practitioner-tracker statistic from an unspecified, probably
short window, with no peer-reviewed study finding a *stable* crew- or
injury-timing effect. The one academic-adjacent source I found for referees
(an Iowa panel study, 2009-2022) reported essentially zero cross-season
stability (-0.04 correlation) for crew-level bias, which argues the loud
crew-level over/under percentages quoted across betting-trend sites are
likely small-sample artifacts that will not survive the next crew reshuffle.
Neither is testable with current data regardless — there is no referee/crew
identifier anywhere in the schema, and the QB-injury claim is fundamentally
about *line-movement timing* (open-to-close reaction speed), which needs a
timestamped odds history this project does not have; only a single closing
snapshot exists.

Rest and travel split cleanly into one decayed/reversed effect and one
outright-refuted effect. Bye-week rest (S031) looks like a textbook case of a
real edge that a rule change killed: pre-2011 CBA, bye teams covered 56.3%
ATS; post-2011 (after the CBA capped padded-practice time, removing the
mechanism that produced the edge), the same angle covers only 48.9%, and a
longer 2002-2025 window puts it at 51.7% — below breakeven. Short-week travel
and jet lag (S033), by contrast, is a case where more rigorous recent methods
reversed an earlier positive finding entirely: Borghesi's 1981-2004
west-to-east travel result was explicitly re-tested with a causal-inference
design on 6,245 college games and came back null (P=.133), with the authors
stating they re-ran two prior NFL jet-lag betting claims and found no
significant effect in replication either. Thursday Night Football unders
(S032) sits in between — directionally consistent across every source (short
week → fewer practice reps → lower scoring) but with hit-rate estimates
ranging from 53.2% to 60.4% depending on which multi-season window is quoted,
which is itself a sign of an angle that has not been tested on one
consistent, pre-registered sample.

Key numbers (S035 spread 3/7, S036 totals 41/43/44/47) are less "is there a
bias" questions and more "is the market already sophisticated about its own
known structure" questions. The one academic test I found (Dare and Holland
2004) is explicit that the closest-performing rule to a key-number edge (home
underdogs) was not statistically significant, and industry pricing sources
independently confirm books now charge extra vig specifically to cross 3 and
7 — i.e., the folk edge that was once real (buy the hook) is reported as
priced away. I found no equivalent formal academic test for the totals-side
key numbers (41/43/44/47), only frequency tables, and one of those tables
shows 44's rank falling from most-common to ~10th-most-common since 2015 —
evidence the clustering itself drifts with the scoring environment, which
undercuts any strategy tuned to a fixed older frequency table.

## Most promising testable entries

- **S027 — wind and totals.** The peer-reviewed mechanism plus a large,
  recent practitioner replication with specific thresholds (15+ mph → 56%),
  and `nfl_total` already carries `wind_mph`. The one thing worth checking
  before trusting a backtest here: whether `wind_mph` in this dataset is a
  pregame forecast or a game-time reading, since the replication's own
  skeptic note is that the edge depends on using the latter — a forecast-only
  column could show a much smaller or vanished effect.

- **S031 — bye-week rest decay.** Directly matches `rest_diff`,
  `home_b2b`/`away_b2b`, and the hidden ATS outcome in `nfl_spread`, and is
  one of the cleaner natural experiments here: a known rule change (2011 CBA)
  with a specific mechanism (less practice time) gives a sharp before/after
  split to test for, rather than a vague "is this still true" question.

- **S028 — cold-temperature refutation.** Cheap to test (`temp_f` plus
  `total_line` plus outcome, all present) and valuable precisely because it
  is a *negative* result worth confirming or overturning on our own data
  before any cold-weather total strategy gets built on the folk assumption.

## What would unlock the untestable entries

- **S029 (rain):** needs a precipitation/rain indicator at kickoff — absent
  from `nfl_total` entirely (only `temp_f`, `wind_mph`, `is_dome`, `roof`,
  `surface` exist).
- **S030 (referee crews):** needs an assigned referee/crew identifier per
  game plus each crew's season roster (crews get reshuffled) — no such column
  exists in any of the three NFL datasets.
- **S033 (travel/jet lag):** needs per-team travel distance and time zones
  crossed, derivable from a static team-city lookup joined to the `home`/
  `away` team codes already present, but that lookup itself is not a column
  today. Low priority to build given the recent causal-inference replication
  found no effect.
- **S034 (backup QB timing):** needs a full open-to-close timestamped odds
  history around injury-report status changes, plus an independent backup-QB
  quality measure — neither exists; `mlb_k_props`-style single closing
  snapshots cannot test a claim that is fundamentally about reaction speed.

## Caveats

- I could not get full-text access to the Borghesi SSRN page or the AABRI
  bettor-biases PDF (both returned 403/socket errors), so the wind and rain
  claimed-edge numbers for S027/S029 lean on secondary summaries (ResearchGate
  abstract text, search-engine synthesis) rather than the primary paper text;
  I cross-checked the win-rate direction and threshold against the
  independent 2025 practitioner replication before trusting it, but flagged
  this lack of primary-source access in each entry rather than quoting a
  precise sample size I could not verify.
- I did not register a separate "home underdog" or general Levitt-bias entry
  despite it surfacing repeatedly in this search (it is the one rule Dare and
  Holland tested against the key-number distribution) — T01 already covers
  both Borghesi's home-underdog finding (S003) and Levitt's exploit hypothesis
  (S009), and this round's new material about it (the Dare and Holland
  non-significance result) is folded into S035's skeptic_note instead of
  spawning a redundant eleventh entry.
