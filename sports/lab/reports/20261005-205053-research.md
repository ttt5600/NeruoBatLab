# T02 — Player prop markets: documented mispricings

## What I did

Searched for player-prop-specific mispricing claims across sources: peer-reviewed/
working papers first, then practitioner write-ups, then bare claims — covering the
under-bias, low-volume/secondary props, role-player and injury-usage cascades, minutes
projections, and MLB strikeout props specifically (the one market we can actually test).
For every claim I looked for a refutation or a reason it might have decayed. Added 9
registry entries (S010–S018).

Important finding about the *tooling*, not the topic: this round, the WebSearch
summarizer invented at least one specific statistic that does not exist in the source
it cited ("5% reduction in expected IP and Ks" on short rest — the actual page it came
from, when fetched directly, contained no such figure, no methodology, and no sample
size, just two unrelated unsourced run-differential numbers). I did not use that figure
anywhere, flagged it explicitly in S014's skeptic_note, and from that point treated every
WebSearch-generated "one study found X%" sentence as unverified until I opened the
underlying page myself. Several did not survive that check (S017's "receiving yards
over-projected by about 4%" claim — I could never find that figure in any real source,
so I excluded it from S017 and said so in its skeptic_note instead of repeating it).

## Overall picture

Player props are a genuinely different animal from the sides/totals studied in T01: there
is close to no peer-reviewed literature on *sportsbook* player-prop pricing specifically.
The two solid peer-reviewed threads I found (Paul/Weinbach/Losak and Baris/Losak) are
about **DFS pricing** (salaries, lineup ownership), not sportsbook odds — a related but
distinct market. Everything that talks about sportsbook player-prop lines directly is
practitioner content, and almost none of it shows a real record or backtest; it's claim-
or anecdote-grade almost across the board (7 of 9 entries graded `claim`/`anecdote`).

The most interesting result is a direct contradiction inside the same small research
group: Losak, Weinbach & Paul (2024/2026, *American Behavioral Scientist*) find MLB
hitters have **no real hot-hand effect** — DFS players believe in it anyway, and the
lineups that win are the ones that fade it (S011). But Paul, Weinbach & Losak (2020,
*Journal of Prediction Markets*) find NBA DFS salaries **under-price a real hot-hand
effect**, and Baris & Losak (2026, *International Journal of Sport Finance*) find the
same for MLB starting pitchers (S012). Same behavioral story, opposite verdict, depending
on sport and player type — a clean illustration of why "hot hand bias" cannot be
registered as one generic cross-sport strategy, and exactly the kind of tension the
standing orders ask me to surface rather than average away.

The structural story practitioners repeat everywhere (S015) is coherent and probably
mostly true as *mechanism* — books post hundreds of props per slate, cannot sharpen all
of them, charge 6–20% hold on secondary/exotic props vs. ~4.5–5% on sides/totals, and
there's no single market-making book for props the way Pinnacle/Circa act for sides
(establishtherun is good on this specifically) — but "wider hold" is also just a rational
price for genuinely higher variance in bench-player outcomes, and no source separates
those two explanations with data. I could not find a single practitioner or academic
source that reports an actual prop-betting record net of the vig.

## Most promising testable entries

- **S013 — MLB strikeout props lag matchup/pitch-mix/umpire factors.** This is the
  entry best matched to data we actually have: `mlb_k_props` ships `mu_model`,
  `p_over_model`, `p_over_mkt`, `ump_k_factor`, and `park_k_factor` as columns, which
  means whoever built this dataset already tried to encode exactly the factors
  practitioners claim the market misses. The real test isn't "do umpires matter" (they
  clearly might, hence the column), it's whether `p_over_model` actually beats
  `p_over_mkt` against realized strikeouts at all.

- **S014 — Short rest / heavy recent workload overprices strikeout overs.** Also
  directly testable with `mlb_k_props` (`rest_days`, `pitches_last3`, `tr_bf`,
  `expected_bf` alongside the model/market probabilities), but graded `anecdote` — this
  is the thinnest-sourced entry in the batch and the one where I caught and excluded a
  fabricated-looking statistic. Worth testing cheaply since the columns are already
  there, but go in expecting modern teams script pitch counts closely enough that there
  may be nothing left for a bettor to find.

- **S011/S012 — the hot-hand pair, if `mlb_k_props` had a recent-form column.** Not
  testable *as registered* (no hot/cold streak feature exists in our schema independent
  of whatever `mu_model` already bakes in), but cheapest to unlock of the untestable
  entries: a rolling last-2-3-start K-rate delta vs. season K-rate, joined to the
  existing `p_over_mkt`/`mu_model`/outcome columns, would let a backtest agent check
  Baris & Losak's pitcher-streak claim against real betting closes rather than DFS
  salaries, which is a genuinely new test nobody in the literature I found has run.

## What would unlock the untestable entries

- **S010 (public over-bias/unders), S015 (low-volume props), S016 (injury cascade),
  S017 (NFL receiving yards), S018 (soccer shots/cards):** all need a player-prop-level
  dataset we simply do not have yet — NBA/NFL player props with odds history (not just
  closing), and ideally ticket-count/handle-percentage by side for S010 specifically,
  since that's the actual mechanism Paul/Weinbach's game-level version of this claim
  depends on.
- **S016** additionally needs timestamped injury/inactive announcements, since the
  claimed edge is about a repricing *lag window*, not the final line.
- **S012 (pitcher hot-hand vs. betting lines)** just needs one new derived column on
  top of `mlb_k_props` (see above) — by far the cheapest unlock in this batch.

## Caveats

Two paper abstracts (Losak/Weinbach/Paul hot-hand DFS paper, Baris/Losak pitcher paper)
were paywalled past the abstract/search-snippet level (403 on direct fetch); I used
publisher/abstract pages and said so in each `skeptic_note` rather than inventing method
or effect-size detail I couldn't see. S014 contains an explicit note that this agent
found and rejected a fabricated-looking statistic rather than passing it through — flagging
this because the same search tool will be used on later topics and the failure mode is
worth watching for generally, not just in this one entry.
